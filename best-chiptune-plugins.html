What makes a good chiptune tool

The sound of late-80s handheld and console music was a hardware budget, not a style choice. A typical sound chip gave a composer about four voices: two pulse waves with a few duty cycles, one small wavetable or triangle voice, and one noise generator. Tuning stepped in coarse increments because pitch lived in a small register. Everything you remember was written inside those walls.

So the test for any chiptune tool is simple - does it actually reproduce the constraints, or does it just add dirt?

  • Pulse waves with switchable duty cycles - the single most recognisable chip sound. Thin duties read as "retro lead", wide ones as square-wave bass.
  • A low-resolution wavetable or triangle voice - the bass and pad register of a chip arrangement.
  • LFSR noise - the shift-register noise generator that did every snare, hat and explosion of the era.
  • Stepped tuning - real chips could not hold arbitrary pitch. That slight out-of-tuneness in the high register is a feature, not sloppiness.
  • Arpeggios - fast one-channel arps were how composers faked chords when they had no channel to spare. A chiptune tool without an arp is missing a core idiom.
  • A workflow you will actually use - the most authentic tool in the world is useless if it lives outside your DAW and you never open it.

The four approaches, and who each is for

Trackers are the original workflow: a pattern editor where you type notes and effect commands into columns, targeting a specific chip. Maximum authenticity, and still the best way to write chip music from nothing. The catch is that they are standalone applications with a learning curve that has nothing to do with how your DAW works.

Chip synth plugins put chip voices in your DAW as an instrument you play from MIDI. You keep your piano roll and your session; you give up some of the exact hardware quirks a dedicated tracker exposes. This is the right default for most producers writing chip parts.

Resynthesisers go the other direction. Instead of you playing a chip, they listen to audio you already have - a vocal, a bass, a hook - track its pitch and envelope, and replay that performance on modelled chip channels. This is the only approach that turns your own take into a chip line.

Bitcrushers and decimators do not synthesise anything. They reduce bit depth and sample rate on whatever passes through, which gets you grit, aliasing and lo-fi texture. Essential for drums and full mixes, where nothing can pitch-track. But a crushed vocal is a crushed vocal - it is not a chip playing your melody. If you only take one thing from this page, take that distinction.

How to make chiptune from your own audio

Everything above assumes you are writing chip music. If instead you have a take you already love - a vocal hook, a bass line, a lead - and you want that performance playing on a chip, none of the trackers or MIDI synths will do it. That is the resynthesis job. Here is the method, using TALLBOY because it is the tool we built for exactly this.

1

Pick a source with one clear pitch

A lead vocal or vocal double, a bass line, a single-note hook, a mono synth part. Pitch tracking needs one fundamental to lock onto - chords, pads and full mixes make any tracker chase partials and warble. That is physics, not a bug. Tighten the take first if it is messy; the chip voice follows what you feed it.

2

Insert the CHIP cartridge and choose a duty

The resynth stage tracks pitch and envelope and replays them on the two pulse channels, the 32-sample 4-bit wavetable and the noise generator. The duty cycle is your main tone control: thin duties give you a nasal retro lead, wide duties sit closer to square-wave bass. Noise is keyed off transients, so consonants in a vocal turn into chip percussion on their own.

3

Add the ARP cartridge for the chip idiom

This is the step that separates "8-bit-ish" from chiptune. Sync the arp to the project - 1/16 UP across two octaves is the classic move - or use CHRD mode to fake harmony from a single monophonic line, which is exactly what chip composers did when they had no spare channel. Glide up to 400 ms turns a stepped arp into a slide instead.

4

Crush to taste, then blend or replace

CRUSH ROM last: modest bit reduction for sheen, hard rate divide with jitter and the post filter pulled down for full cartridge dirt. Then decide what the part is. Fully wet, it is a new instrument playing your melody. Blended 20-40% under the original, it is an 8-bit double that makes a hook feel twice as wide. For drums, eject CHIP and ARP and run CRUSH ROM alone - that is the honest way to get chip-adjacent percussion.

Common questions

What is the best free chiptune plugin?

It depends what you are doing. To write chip parts from MIDI, a free chip-style synth instrument is the fastest route. For maximum authenticity, a free open-source tracker gets you closest to real hardware, at the cost of learning a pattern editor. If you only want crunch and texture on audio you already have, a free bitcrusher is enough. There is no single answer because these are not the same job.

Do I need a tracker to make chiptune?

No. Trackers are the authentic route and still the best tools for writing chip music from scratch, but they are standalone applications with their own workflow. If you already work in a DAW, a chip synth plugin or a resynthesiser fits your session without changing how you write.

Is a bitcrusher enough to make chiptune?

No. A bitcrusher reduces bit depth and sample rate, degrading the audio you already have. That is lo-fi grit, not the sound of a chip playing your melody. Chiptune character comes from pulse waves, a small wavetable, hardware noise and stepped tuning - which needs synthesis or resynthesis, not degradation. Bitcrusher vs decimator covers the difference in detail.

Can I turn my own vocal or bass line into chiptune?

Yes - that is what a resynthesiser is for. It pitch-tracks a monophonic source and replays the performance on modelled chip channels, so your own take drives the chip voice. It needs one clear pitch at a time, so vocals, bass lines and single-note leads work; chords and full mixes do not. See the 8-bit vocal effect guide for the vocal version specifically.

Will these work in my DAW?

The plugins here load in any host that takes standard plugin formats. The trackers do not - they are standalone applications, and you bounce audio out of them and import it. That difference matters more than any feature list when you are choosing, so decide up front whether you want something inside your session or beside it.
